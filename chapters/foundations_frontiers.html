Introduction

Cities are physical archives of conscious spatial decisions and countless unintended events. When we walk the street, when we navigate with a map in our hands, or when we read about a place as visitors, we can see glimpses of this rich history. As professionals, we need to do research on urban form in a more systematic way, but essentially, we use the same techniques: we read maps, we document how a city is experienced at eye level, and we look for additional supporting data. In this way, we try to uncover hidden spatial patterns in a city. This study we call Urban Morphology and it remains an essential item in the urbanist’s toolbox.

In this open guidebook on Urban Morphology, we set out to trace the foundations and frontiers of the field and, building on that, to provide urbanism students with a state-of-the-art overview on how to employ urban morphology analytical methods. We hope this will allow for a better understanding of the rules governing urban development but also help to make better design decisions.

Foundations

Morphology literally means the science of form. The term has its roots in early nineteenth century botany and atonomy. Researchers had already proposed taxonomies of species based on the physical characteristics of plants and animals, but others found this approach to be missing an essential point: there may be countless species, but they appear to share important formal or growth charateristics.  To uncover these, one should study many examples of plants or animals, but an equally important step is to build a mental image of what they all have in common to understand the shared principle of growth. This mental abstraction was called ‘type’. A type does not exist in reality but can be utilised as a proxy to better understand real events. This new science of natural development was coined ‘Morphology’.

In post-WWII urbanism, morphology and the concept of type became key terms1. As in nature, the assumption was that historically, urban development is governed by general and comparable principles of growth and structure. To uncover these, urbanists need to do empirical research into the physical form and development of cities and conceptualise these into types. This will allow designers to make better decisions regarding future development in at least two ways: historical lines of development can be extended in a design proposal, limiting the risk of rupture and thus failure. Second, applying the structural rules embedded within a type in a new design prevents designers from drifting too far off from proven and locally successful spatial solutions.

The promotion of historical continuity, structural integrity and a contextualised approach was seen as extremely important by a new generation of planners, designers and geographers in the nineteen fifties and sixties in a critique of modern planning and design practice. This latter approach, based on a small set of universal spatial principles and backed by scientific projections of, for instance, demographics and economics, had led to many problematic urban extensions and the violation of existing cities. Urban Morphology pioneers countered this development by carefully documenting the spatial qualities of historical cities. By abstracting these in analytical maps and drawings, this knowledge also became available for designers and planners in an attempt to safeguard spatial and historical continuity.

The history of urban morphology and its different schools has been documented extensively, and we don’t have to repeat this here. Although Urban Morphology originated as a critique of modern planning practice, many of its concepts and techniques are still being used today, regardless of their original critical intent. In this Open Urban Analysis Guidebook Morphology, we propose to focus here on three research techniques running through much of the urban morphology literature: map-based analysis, perception-based analysis and data-based analysis. These strands can be read as kinds of analysis that share some common characteristics and position towards urban space and form.

Map-based analysis

We choose map-based analysis as one of our basic categories because for many students and practitioners in urbanism, maps and aerial photographs are the most readily available sources of information. For urbanism professionals, maps are also conventional as a mode of communication and often acquire legal status. Map-based methods are characterised by a strong choice of what is and is not shown, and how (color, contrast, annotations, hierarchies, etc.). This means that maps are inherently political in nature. They are visually powerful and can reveal actual development patterns, but they may also lie or misrepresent. Designers should be aware of this and be careful not to misuse their talents in producing drawings. So, producing maps requires both cartographic knowledge and skills [ref. to key cartography resources] as well as an ethical stance to space production that is often overlooked.

Key examples:

  • Map-based analysis is a deeply rooted approach going back to Nolli’s figure-ground analysis of Rome;

  • The Dutch tradition originating in the late seventies, early eighties. Designers like Rein Geurtsen and Maurits de Hoog;

  • Recent examples include Frits Palmboom’s book ‘Drawing the Ground’ or ‘Barcelona: the Urban Evolution of a Compact City’ by Joan Busquets.

Perception-based analysis

This type of analysis characterises urban space through the eyes of a person (or, why not, a non-human agent) who navigates or uses it. It typically uses eye-level perspectives to systematically (e.g., sequentially) analyse the perception of urban space. Those perspectives can be photographs which are qualitatively analysed, as well as abstractions of reality such as a hand sketch (qualitative) or segmented street view image (quantitative). The perception information can also be further abstracted into mind maps following a method like Lynch’s, for instance. Such abstract map-based notation of course has elements of a map-based analysis, but it remains fundamentally perceptual and subject to interpretation (i.e., two people can draw considerably different maps of the same space). This method will benefit from a good understanding of environmental behaviour and cognition.

Key examples:

  • Kevin Lynch, Gordon Cullen

  • Serial vision, scenic approach, human cognition, environmental psychology,

  • The degree to which a city can be ‘read’ by its users. Open, closed, light, dark, contrasts, how ‘scenes’ are linked through sight lines and other visual clues. Mapping the status quo and then deciding how to improve these.

Data-based analysis

Data-based analyses leverage data, either by combining a large variety of datasets (e.g., in an integrated local analysis) or by extracting patterns from large amounts of data. The main difference from the other two types of analysis is that they answer questions which could not be answered, or at least not fully answered, without data. Typical methods in this category include Space Syntax analysis, quantitative typology analysis, and various other spatial applications of machine learning (e.g., classification of building types based on footprints, prediction of new urban growth locations). These methods also require data analytical skills, including GIS and spatial statistics. We have included data based analysis as a basic category in this book because the desire to quantify urban phenomena has been part of urban research from the early twentieth century onward and continues to attract students. Urban data forever carries with it the promise of a solid, undisputable basis for design. But as with maps, data can be misunderstood or even misused. Data literacy therefore is not complete without a proper awareness of the limits of knowledge to be extracted from data and how to use this knowledge responsibly.

Key examples:

  • Space Syntax
  • Fleischmann
  • Biljecki

Frontiers

The three strands of urban morphology have seen considerable methodological and theoretical developments influenced by (1) the increasing availability and diversification of spatial and non-spatial data, computational capacity and related methods and tools, (2) growing attention to sustainability and a maturing body of knowledge on the relationship between spatial/physical form and urban phenomena. Contemporary map-based, perception-based, and data-based urban morphology analyses are increasingly global, evidence-based, collective, and detailed. Methods that were previously limited to a few cases (in the tens) can now be conducted at scale across hundreds or thousands of cases. This has deep implications for the kinds of questions one can ask and the depth of insights one can derive.

New data, methods and tools

An important enabling factor of urban morphology is the increasing availability of open data products. The emergence of Volunteered Geographic Information, most notably through OpenStreetMap (2025), has made urban form data globally available. OpenStreetMap features such as streets, buildings, and points of interest are available with increasing global coverage. Where such data is not available, datasets are generated using machine learning techniques applied to satellite imagery, as in Google’s Open Buildings project (Sirko et al. 2021). Moreover, LiDAR (point-cloud) data provides high-resolution 3D information on building, street and landscape levels—the Dutch digital elevation model AHN4 (2020-2022) is derived from such data. Street-view imagery is also increasingly released as open data products. For instance, the availability of Mapillary data has enabled the creation of the Global Streetscapes dataset (Biljecki et al., 2025). Also, derived products such as segmented street view imagery (i.e., images in which objects and other visible elements or characteristics of a scene have been classified with computer vision) are increasingly made available openly. Moreover, when data is not openly available, methods exist to generate proxies computationally, with a notable example of Voronoi-based partitioning used as a proxy for parcel data (Fleischmann et al., 2020).

The emergence and increasing availability of data have also led to the development of new tools for processing and analysing those data. Advanced analytical tools are increasingly open and accessible to anyone. Open-source software such as the urban morphology toolkit momepy (Fleischmann, 2019) has enabled automated and scalable workflows. OSMnx (Boeing, 2025) and sfnetworks (van der Meer et al., 2024) allow for programmatic acquisition, processing and analysis of spatial network data. Tools like the street geometry processing toolkit neatnet (Gaboardi et al., 2026) enable the simplification of complex data prior to analysis. While all these tools have made programmatic urban morphology accessible to a growing user base, graphical user interfaces remain the community’s primary choice. Accordingly, the QGIS platform (Graser et al., 2025) and its rapidly evolving ecosystem of plugins have revolutionised spatial analysis and, as part of that, enabled much of contemporary urban morphology analysis. Many of the programmatic tools mentioned above are becoming available as plugins in QGIS’s point-and-click interface.

Such data products, computational methods and tools enable novel research questions, which can lead to both a better understanding of the current situation and potential transformations of an urban space without having to visit it.

Evolving domains of knowledge and application

As shown in Figure #, urban morphology literature shows two major clusters, or domains of knowledge and application, one mostly centred on and extending traditional urban morphology (in red), and one focused on environmental aspects (in green and blue). We elaborate on those two domains in the following two sections.

Urban morphometrics

The red cluster represents the longstanding efforts of the field to characterise patterns of urban form, mostly centred on traditional typo-morphology, which is more architectural, fundamental, form-based, and applied to fields of mobility/movement, social behaviour, and spatial perception. With a fundamental focus on form, it is also more design-based than the other cluster. Extending that core, the already half-century-old Space Syntax and quantitative typology-based approaches stand out as the most recent additions. We can refer to this as a quantification of the field of urban morphology, increasingly known as urban morphometrics. While rooted in the form-based cluster, it could also provide a solid basis for the environmental cluster, enhancing large-scale cross-case analysis. Compared with environmental urban morphology approaches, urban morphometrics lacks the third vertical dimension and thus is mostly limited to a two-dimensional understanding of traditional elements of urban form, namely streets, plots, blocks, and buildings.

Environmental urban morphology

The second, larger cluster has emerged recently, continues to grow at an increasing pace and is mostly driven by environmental challenges, such as the urban heat island effect, wind corridors, etc., caused by certain spatial configurations. Materiality and non-built elements also play an important role in the more engineering-focused approach dominating this cluster. This is mostly driven by sustainability challenges, which have been increasingly prominent in urban development agendas. This is known in the literature as environmental urban morphology (Maretto et al., 2023).

The environmental domain is focused on local, computationally intensive approaches. These often use detailed 3D models of the environment that try to capture the complex interplay among factors such as wind, precipitation, and temperature. Assessment of current urban compositions and configurations, and their potential for transformation towards a specific target, plays an important role in this cluster. Spatial Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (S-MCDA, ref.), spatial typologies augmented with non-spatial environmental performance information, or more advanced machine learning models meant to classify spatial elements or to predict specific environmental performances are commonly employed in this field.

Quality and data literacy

While these recent developments make the field more quantitative, qualitative input (deciding on what to analyse and why) and interpretation (what do the results mean) remain essential. For instance, clustering-based approaches, increasingly used to construct quantitative typologies, require both well-informed input (a meaningful number of clusters) and critical interpretation (what do the clusters mean?). Also, with the increasing availability of real and proxy data, the issue is no longer a lack of information but potentially too much of it. Not least, a theoretical understanding of urban form and its interactions with both social and environmental phenomena remains crucial in quantitative approaches. These issues can only be overcome qualitatively and require critical data literacy.

The analytical skills of an urbanist need to incorporate these recent developments. To what extent, though? What foundational knowledge is necessary for a student of urban morphology to navigate data-augmented contemporary practice of urbanism with competence and confidence? Can we expect urban designers to be experts in machine learning, for instance? What should students learn in the university, and what can they safely encounter only in practice? How do students and teachers position themselves in relation to recent data-producing and data-analytical technologies?

Morphological analysis and design

For some students, performing urban analysis is perceived as a waste of time because it is unclear how the results of an analysis can be used to improve or even generate design ideas. Conversely, some students will protract the analysis phase unnecessarily as research appears to be less arbitrary than design.

One of the purposes of this book is to provide students in architecture and urbanism with a stronger basis to develop their design ideas on. In this paragraph, we will try to explain how analysis can be used as a powerful design tool.

For the purpose of this book, we say that morphological analysis is a necessary step in the design process. Above, we stated that morphological analysis helps design in at least two ways. It reveals historical growth patterns which an uninformed design may easily break. Also, it unveils typological rules which can safeguard structural integrity in a new design. Seen from this perspective, morphological and typological analysis both support spatial continuity. They have a tendency to sustain the existing, which follows from the critical attitude of its main protagonists. But even if a designer would choose to break with history or existing formal structures, this still necessitates knowledge of these structures. The uninformed alternative – we would argue – is folly and means that a designer has no way of predicting the effects a design proposal might have on its spatial context.

How can morphological analysis be effectively applied in urban design?

This is very straightforward. Morphological analysis reveals spatial patterns or typological rules. We can check the spatial patterns for consistency and use a new design to strengthen or complement these. Similarly, if we distill typological rules from a given location, we can apply these in a new design.

To do this effectively, it is important to use a strong legend. In his famous book ‘The Image of the City’ from 1960, Kevin Lynch introduced five points which he argued are necessary for the design of urban spaces: paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks. In drawing maps of Boston with this legend, he was able to see where these needed strengthening. In one of the drawings “Problems of the Boston Image”, Lynch pointed at locations with for instance “direction ambiguity” or “characterless path”, which derive from the original legend. Even though there are no designs included in the book, it is clear now what a designer might do to improve the situation. In the appropriate locations, it must become less ambiguous for people to orient themselves, or a path must acquire a more distinctive spatial character. From this example, we learn that an effective step from analysis to design requires the same legend for both. If we analyse paths and edges, it is because we believe that these are important parameters for design. So think carefully about the items in your legend before you start an analysis, for these are the items you will be working with in your design.

Another example is Gordon Cullen’s famous illustration of what he called ‘serial vision’. Reading his book ‘Townscape’ from 1961, it becomes clear that highlighting sequences of exposure and enclosure, of constraint and relief, and contrasts in scale, style and texture function as ‘legend’ to Cullen’s drawings. Each drawing shows a clear spatial scene, but also reveals what lies ahead in an emerging view. A person moving through a city will constantly look for these visual clues on how to move forward. In the example in the book, these contrasts are particularly strong and clear. Again, if analytical drawings like these reveal that an existing view does not reveal the next step, a designer may work to enhance this through a manipulation of the spatial sequence using contrasts in scale and texture to strengthen exposure.

In data based analyses, Christopher Alexander’s patterns were originally part of a design method where parameters were linked to spatial patterns, which would then be combined into a larger, more complex design. This linear approach has been abandoned. In this book we propose to use data-driven analysis techniques mostly as a tool to evaluate multiple design proposals in a quantitative way. A plan analysis. Data-based analyses can be used not only to describe the current situation but also to make predictions or assess a possible transformation relative to it. How would a new bridge hereimprove safety in this neighbourhood? Which routes could benefit most from this transformed street profile? These are examples of questions that can be answered with a data-based method. As a quantitative approach, this entails making assumptions explicit and working with probabilities. Also, most methods are rather evaluative than generative, meaning that design solutions are not created through data but are rather informed by data-based analysis; the designer is still in the driver’s seat.

This chapter explores emerging frontiers in urban morphology: urban morphometrics, spatial morphology, and spatial data science. Urban morphometrics is a quantitative field aiming at extracting patterns from large amounts of morphological data; spatial morphology expands the field to the analysis of unbuilt space mostly using space syntax theory and landscape ecology principles; spatial data science as a cross-cutting field increasingly adopts urban morphological information to enrich the understanding of spatial phenomena of environmental, social and economic kind.
